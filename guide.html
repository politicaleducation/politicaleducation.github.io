How to read weapon names

Most names are shorthand. They usually compress some mix of year, factory designation, caliber, or role. Once you learn the naming habits, the labels become much less mysterious.

Names do not reliably tell you everything. A carbine can fire the same cartridge as a rifle. A “light machine gun” can look like a big rifle. An “assault rifle” is mainly defined by its role and cartridge class, not by looking scary or having a particular stock.

How caliber labels work

A label like 5.56×45 means roughly: bullet diameter by case length, both in millimeters. The first number is not a promise that all cartridges with the same diameter are interchangeable. The second number matters, the case shape matters, the pressure matters, and the chamber matters.

Fast rule of thumb

9×19 is a pistol cartridge. 5.56×45, 5.45×39, and 7.62×39 are intermediate rifle cartridges. 7.62×51 NATO and 7.62×54R are full-power rifle and machine-gun cartridges. In the cards above, the measurements are the point: bullet diameter, case length, and overall cartridge length.

What the role labels mean

These labels describe battlefield role more than they describe one exact shape. The same family can appear in several roles by changing barrel length, stock, feed system, and intended use.

Close-range

SMG

Submachine gun. Usually fires pistol ammunition like 9×19 mm. Good for compactness and controllability at short range.

  • Examples: MP5, Uzi, PPSh-41
  • Main clue: pistol cartridge
  • Modern role shrank once short carbines became common
General infantry

Assault rifle

Select-fire rifle using an intermediate cartridge. This category became the standard infantry weapon class after World War II.

  • Examples: M16, M4, AK-47, AK-74, QBZ-95
  • Main clue: intermediate rifle round
  • Can fire semi-auto and full-auto or burst
Shorter rifle

Carbine

Usually a shorter, handier version of a rifle. A carbine can use the same ammunition as the longer rifle version.

  • Example: M4 as a shorter branch of the M16 family
  • Main clue: shorter barrel / compact role
  • Not a separate ammo category by itself
Sustained squad fire

LMG / SAW

Light machine gun, often the squad automatic weapon. Intended to provide longer bursts and suppressive fire for a small unit.

  • Examples: M249, RPK, QJB-95
  • Often bipod-equipped
  • May be belt-fed or magazine-fed
Heavier support

GPMG

General-purpose machine gun. Usually uses a full-power rifle cartridge and can serve on bipod, tripod, or vehicle mount.

  • Examples: PKM, M240
  • Longer reach and heavier ammunition
  • Above the squad-rifle class in weight and role
Older standard

Battle rifle

Rifle firing a full-power cartridge instead of an intermediate one. Common before assault rifles took over standard infantry service.

  • Examples: M14, FN FAL, G3
  • More recoil and weight
  • Better reach, worse controllability in automatic fire

Timeline of the shift to modern infantry weapons

The big story is the move from full-power rifles toward lighter intermediate-cartridge weapons, then toward optics, modular accessories, and shorter carbines without abandoning the assault-rifle role.

1940s

Intermediate cartridges become central

The late World War II period shows why most infantry fighting happens at ranges that do not demand old full-power rifle ammunition. The Soviet answer eventually becomes the AK-47 in 7.62×39.

1950s

China copies and scales Soviet patterns

China begins Type 56 production using Soviet technical help. This matters because it spreads AK- and SKS-type weapons far beyond Soviet borders.

1960s

The U.S. commits to 5.56

The M16 family marks the U.S. turn toward a lighter, high-velocity small-caliber service rifle. The Soviets later answer with their own smaller round, 5.45×39.

1970s

AK-74 shrinks the bullet, not the concept

The AK-74 keeps the Kalashnikov operating concept but moves to a lighter, faster round. This parallels the broader global trend toward smaller intermediate cartridges.

1980s–1990s

Squad automatic weapons and compact carbines

The M249 becomes the U.S. SAW in 1984. The M4 arrives in the 1990s as a shorter carbine branch of the M16 family. China adopts the QBZ-95 in 1997.

2000s–today

Modularity and optics dominate

The modern story is less about inventing a completely new class and more about mounting optics, rails, lights, suppressors, improved magazines, and refined ammunition on existing families.

One page mental model

If you only remember a few things, remember these.

1. Cartridge class usually drives the category

SMGs are usually pistol-caliber. Assault rifles use intermediate cartridges. Battle rifles and GPMGs use full-power cartridges. That one rule explains a lot of seemingly random names.

2. Shorter gun does not automatically mean weaker cartridge

An M4 is shorter than an M16, but both are 5.56×45. “Carbine” often means shorter and handier, not a fundamentally different ammunition family.

3. AK and AR are families, not single frozen objects

Once you stop imagining one iconic picture and start thinking in branches, the history gets easier. There are rifle versions, carbine versions, and support-weapon cousins.

4. China begins inside the Soviet tree, then exits it

Type 56 is the copy era. QBZ-95 is the domestic-system era. That is the simplest way to understand the Chinese side of the story.

Common beginner confusions

These are the points that trip people up most often.

Why are both 7.62×39 and 7.62×54R called 7.62 if they are so different?

Because the first number is only the approximate bullet diameter. The case length, case shape, pressure, and overall cartridge design are different. Sharing a diameter number does not make cartridges interchangeable.

Why is the M4 called a carbine if it fires rifle ammo?

Because “carbine” usually describes the weapon format, not the ammunition category. The M4 is a shorter, more compact branch of the M16 family but still uses 5.56×45 rifle ammunition.

Is the RPK just an AK with a longer barrel?

That is the right first approximation. It is a squad automatic branch of the Kalashnikov family: heavier barrel, bipod, and role changes for sustained fire, but still closely tied to the AK system.

Why did both the U.S. and Soviets move toward smaller rounds?

Weight, controllability, and practical combat range. Infantry can carry more ammunition, recoil is easier to manage, and automatic or rapid semi-automatic fire becomes more practical.

Did China keep using Soviet calibers forever?

No. China spent decades with Soviet-derived 7.62×39 weapons, then moved to the indigenous 5.8×42 cartridge with the QBZ-95 family.
